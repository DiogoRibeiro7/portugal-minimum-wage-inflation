\section{Long-run evidence}
\label{sec:results}

\input{tables/headline_macros}

This section reports the historical accounting layer. It is descriptive. It
documents what the statutory wage did relative to a productivity-and-inflation
benchmark, and motivates the identification strategy; it is not itself evidence
of a causal effect of minimum wages on prices.

\subsection{The benchmark and the residual}

For year $t$ the benchmark nominal wage growth compounds productivity growth
with the previous year's inflation,
%
\begin{equation}
  g^{\text{benchmark}}_{t} = (1 + g^{A}_{t})(1 + \pi_{t-1}) - 1,
  \qquad
  r_{t} = g^{MW}_{t} - g^{\text{benchmark}}_{t},
  \label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
%
where $r_t$ is the policy residual. The benchmark compounds rather than adds.
The distinction is immaterial at recent inflation rates and is not immaterial
over the sample: with inflation near thirty per cent, as in the early 1980s, the
additive approximation understates the benchmark by more than a percentage
point, which exceeds most of the residuals being measured.

\subsection{The wage floor against productivity}

Figure~\ref{fig:real-wage-productivity} indexes the real minimum wage and output
per worker to \MacroFirstYear{}. Over the \ObservedYears{} years to
\MacroLastYear{}, productivity reached \ProductivityIndexEnd{} while the real
minimum wage reached \RealWageIndexEnd{}.

The divergence is the central historical fact. The real value of the wage floor
fell sharply through the inflation of the late 1970s and early 1980s and did not
regain its \MacroFirstYear{} level for four decades. Measured against
productivity, the ratio bottomed at \WageToProductivityMin{} in
\WageToProductivityMinYear{} and stood at \WageToProductivityEnd{} at the end of
the sample: the minimum wage buys a far smaller share of what an average worker
produces than it did when it was introduced.

Three periods stand out. Through the revolutionary transition and its aftermath
the statutory wage rose quickly in nominal terms and yet fell far behind a
benchmark driven by inflation above twenty per cent a year; the residual is
strongly negative and the real wage floor lost roughly a third of its value.
From the late 1990s the residual is close to zero: the wage floor broadly
tracked productivity and prices. From 2015 the sign reverses, and the residual
turns persistently positive, which is the policy shift the identification
strategy is designed to exploit.

\subsection{What this does not establish}

The residual is an accounting object. A positive residual means the wage floor
rose faster than the benchmark; it does not mean the increase was inflationary,
and the correlation between the residual and subsequent inflation cannot settle
the question, because statutory changes respond to the same conditions that
drive prices. That is precisely why the causal layer uses predetermined exposure
and panel variation rather than the national time series, and why no
pass-through estimate is reported until the regional coverage data required by
that design is available.
