\begin{abstract}
We assemble the statutory history of the Portuguese minimum wage from its
introduction in May 1974 to 2026 from the official register of acts, with the
autonomous regions' schedules read from primary law, and use it to document the
long-run relationship between the wage floor, consumer prices and labour
productivity. Three results follow. First, the real value of the minimum
wage fell sharply through the inflation of the late 1970s and early 1980s and
did not regain its 1974 level for four decades, while output per worker more
than doubled: measured against productivity, the wage floor reached barely a
third of its introductory value before recovering to about half. Second,
decomposing statutory growth against a benchmark that compounds productivity
growth with prior inflation shows a sign reversal, from a large negative
residual in the revolutionary period to a persistently positive one from 2015.
Third, we show that the regional variation available in Portugal cannot
identify the price pass-through of minimum-wage policy, and document why: the
Azorean supplement is proportional and so contributes no independent timing,
Madeira is the only region legislating its own value, and with the small number
of Portuguese regions conventional cluster-robust inference rejects a true null
far above its nominal size. We report the estimates that conventional inference
would call significant, the bootstrap that leaves one of seven horizons, and the
step-down correction across the horizon family that leaves none. We also
build the shift-share exposure measure that would substitute for regional
policy variation, and show that its spread across the \ExposureRegions{}
NUTS~II regions is too narrow to identify anything --- a constraint of the
Portuguese wage distribution rather than a gap in the data. The
statutory series, its regional schedules, and the full pipeline are released.
\end{abstract}
